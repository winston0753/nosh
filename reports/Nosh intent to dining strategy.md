# Nosh intent to dining strategy

October 28, 2026 · Strategy proposal, not an accepted product change

**Nosh’s strongest direction is helping people turn restaurants they already want to try into meals they actually have.** Start with a few explicit wants, one feasible meal, and an optional invitation to someone they already know. This reduces dependence on years of review history while preserving Nosh’s useful premise: remove work at the moment a decision matters. Your proximity idea fits this direction, but an open marketplace of nearby strangers would introduce a difficult new problem: finding enough compatible, available people at the same time. The mealtime nudge is a promising entry point, not yet a moat. The defensibility hypothesis is a trusted routine that repeatedly produces completed meals with very little effort.

The documentation already separates part of this problem correctly. D021, D022 and D027 replace the curated restaurant catalog with live Google Places and retain provider identifiers plus Nosh-owned data. Nosh has already decided against maintaining its own comprehensive restaurant database. The remaining burden is reconstructing personal visit history and learning taste. D025 reduced onboarding work, but the recommendation policy still includes learned cuisine preference, and the handoff proposes an additional cuisine question after meals. Those requirements deserve reconsideration before further investment. Three different resources are being conflated under “owning our data.” Restaurant facts—identity, location, hours and price—remain provider inputs. Historical taste and visit records are costly to collect and maintain. **Current intent, availability and commitment can be useful immediately:** “I want to try this,” “I have 40 minutes,” and “I can join you.” A product built around those signals does not need to beat Yelp at reviews or Beli at accumulated taste history. It still needs accurate place resolution and feasible suggestions, but it can deliver value before asking for extensive backfill.

That opportunity is narrower than “a wishlist with reminders.” Beli advertises want-to-try lists, friend activity, taste profiles and friend match scores. Google Maps supports shared lists. Mapstr explicitly advertises proximity alerts for saved “To try” places. These are verified advertised capabilities, not evidence of their effectiveness or an exhaustive feature audit. They mean Nosh needs a sharper promise than saving and resurfacing restaurants. ([Beli](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/beli/id1478375386), [Google Maps](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/7280933?co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop&hl=en), [Mapstr](https://en.mapstr.com/faq))

The proposed promise is: **one wanted restaurant, a workable time, and an easy way to commit.** The competitor is also the existing group chat. If Nosh simply moves “where should we eat?” into another app, it creates work. It wins only if it removes enough negotiation to make a meal happen.

Your shared-wishlist idea becomes more practical within existing relationships. Imagine two coworkers explicitly indicating at 11:15 that they are free for lunch near the office. Nosh proposes a restaurant one of them saved, a 12:10 departure, and an acceptance deadline. Both agree; the plan is settled. If the other person declines, the original user still has a useful solo suggestion. These times are illustrative, not an established optimal schedule.

**Exact wishlist overlap should be a bonus, not a requirement.** Two people can happily eat together even if only one saved the restaurant. Requiring everyone to have independently bookmarked the same place excludes many good outcomes. A stronger rule is that one person wants it, everyone accepts it, and everyone’s practical constraints fit. Rotating whose wish gets fulfilled can make the experience feel fair without building a sophisticated group taste model.

Nearby strangers are a materially different bet. Geographic overlap, simultaneous availability, food compatibility, budget, willingness to meet and reciprocal commitment must align. Restaurant overlap adds another constraint. Trust, unwanted contact, cancellations and no-shows also become product responsibilities. Timeleft illustrates a different approach: it gathers explicit date and neighborhood preferences and organizes scheduled groups. That concentrates demand over time; it does not prove instant nearby matching will work. ([Timeleft](https://timeleft.com/how-it-works/))

The existing privacy design also matters. Nosh uses foreground location and location-free nudges. It cannot silently know who is nearby at noon when those people have not opened the app. A compatible proposal is an expiring declaration such as “free for lunch near this area until 1,” with mutual agreement on a meeting place. Social sharing, invitations and group permissions are currently outside phase one and would require an explicit product decision. Five directions deserve consideration, in this order of fit for the current product and learning value. These are strategic judgments, not validated demand estimates.

**First: activate a tiny shortlist.** Ask for three places someone would realistically visit this week, then offer one when time, budget and location fit. This has the fewest dependencies and tests whether neglected intent is valuable. Avoid requiring users to migrate their entire collection: that can recreate the onboarding burden you are trying to escape. Bulk import and sync capabilities have not been established for Nosh; a few manually chosen places are enough for the experiment.

**Second: a lunch buddy inside an existing circle.** Offer “I’m going here at 12:10—join?” to a friend or coworker. Begin in one office or coworking community where proximity and trust already exist. The recipient should be able to respond through the existing conversation during a manual trial, without installing another app. The central uncertainty is whether a concrete invitation helps more than a solo suggestion.

**Third: a weekly ‘finally try it’ ritual.** A couple or friend pair chooses one outing from their saved places each week. This challenges the assumption that the right moment is always mealtime: by the time people are hungry, plans, packed lunches and commitments can already be fixed. Test a prompt the day before alongside a same-day prompt. Keep this a planning experiment rather than expanding into reservations or calendar access.

**Fourth: an active shortlist organized by occasion.** A saved restaurant can mean “quick lunch,” “with a friend,” or “special dinner.” A small amount of context could prevent Nosh repeatedly offering an aspirational dinner destination during a weekday break. The risk is another organizational chore. Test one lightweight question when a place becomes active, rather than asking users to classify their whole collection.

**Fifth: execute a trusted person’s recommendation.** A friend gives you one or three places and Nosh helps you actually try them. This borrows confidence from an existing relationship without requiring a public reviewer network. It is weaker as a standalone business proposition, but it could seed a shortlist for someone who has no saved places and offer a natural invitation loop. Avoid turning it into another feed to browse.

Across these ideas, the unproven assumption is that forgetting or lack of company causes the backlog. Some saves are aspirational; others are blocked by cost, distance, reservations or the wrong occasion. Neither a notification nor a matching algorithm fixes all of those. Before building, ask prospective users why their last three saved restaurants remain unvisited, and distinguish real intent from casual bookmarking.

**Run a small, consenting, two-week manual pilot before committing to a pivot.** Recruit roughly 12–20 people, including several existing pairs in one lunch area, and collect three realistic wants each. The sample is a practical learning cohort, not a statistically powered study. Record whether even that setup creates abandonment. Compare comparable meal opportunities with a concrete solo nudge and with an optional companion invitation; include some agreed no-nudge opportunities if feasible. A small pilot can reveal patterns, but it cannot establish causal lift just because visits occur after messages.

Measure confirmed meals, repeat voluntary use, coordination effort, declined proposals, no-shows and requests to stop prompts. Keep unknown outcomes unknown. Ask whether the person would otherwise have gone, while treating that answer as imperfect self-report. Suggested continuation criteria are that several people use the service again without founder chasing, meals happen across more than one pair, and participants actively want it to continue. Set any numerical threshold beforehand as a founder decision, not an industry benchmark. If solo use repeats and invitations add little, stay solo. If company drives outcomes but common saves rarely matter, prioritize making plans. If people express enthusiasm but do not act on feasible wants, investigate that before polishing ranking.

The roadmap currently defers notifications to Milestone 4 while substantial foundation and ranking work comes first. That delays testing the proposed differentiator. A manual pilot can test the behavior without silently bypassing the existing implementation order. Production changes should follow a documented decision. Much of the current foundation remains useful: authentication, schedules, price ceilings, reliable responses, session handling, visit confirmation and deletion. This direction does not justify a backend rewrite. ([ARCHITECTURE.md](/Users/winstontang/Downloads/nosh/ARCHITECTURE.md), [IMPLEMENTATION_HANDOFF.md](/Users/winstontang/Downloads/nosh/IMPLEMENTATION_HANDOFF.md))

If the pivot is chosen, propose **confirmed wanted meals completed per active user** as the primary outcome, retaining positive new discoveries as a separate measure and satisfaction as a guardrail. Returning to a restaurant to accompany a friend can be a successful coordinated meal even though it fails today’s strict novelty metric. Preserve Go as intent, never proof of attendance. The attractive long-term asset would be reliable knowledge of which intentions turn into action, gained through useful service rather than extensive onboarding. Whether that produces retention, defensibility or willingness to pay remains open; the next investment should buy evidence about those questions.

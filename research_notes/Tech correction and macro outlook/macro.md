# US macroeconomics and midterms

## What does the latest verified macro data say?

### Takeaway
As of November 2, 2026, the official record supports strong aggregate output alongside weak hiring, squeezed real household income and inflation that limits monetary relief. This is not evidence of an already established recession, nor does it establish a benign all-clear.

### Cited Findings
- **GDP, October 29 release covering Q3:** real GDP +3.4% seasonally adjusted annual rate (SAAR), after Q2 +2.2%; real final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9% SAAR after +4.6%. Q3 GDP was +0.8% at the actual quarterly rate. Investment and government spending accelerated; exports declined. Information-processing equipment supported equipment growth. This is an **advance estimate**, with second estimate November 25. Q3 headline/core PCE price inflation was +2.1%/+2.5% SAAR; these are quarterly rates, **not** September year-over-year inflation. [BEA original release, published October 29, 2026](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/gdp-advance-estimate-3rd-quarter-2026).
- **CPI, October 14 release covering September:** headline +0.6% month over month seasonally adjusted, +3.7% year over year unadjusted; August headline +3.4% year over year. Core +0.3% month over month and +2.6% year over year. Gasoline +9.1% month over month contributed over half the headline monthly increase; energy +5.2% month over month. Do not substitute headline CPI for the Fed's PCE target. [BLS dated original release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm).
- **PCE, October 29 release covering September:** headline prices +0.5% month over month/+3.6% year over year; core +0.3%/+3.1%. Real disposable personal income -0.3% month over month, real consumption rounded 0.0% (under +0.1% in text), saving rate 3.8%. Nominal spending +0.5% does not mean real demand +0.5%. This release updates July/August estimates. Next release November 25. [BEA dated original release](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-september-2026).
- **Employment, October 2 release covering September:** payrolls +29,000; unemployment 4.2% vs August 4.1%. July/August combined payroll revisions -60,000. October employment report is scheduled for **November 6**, so October payrolls are not known as of November 2. [BLS original release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_10022026.htm).

### Inferences
- GDP is inconsistent with a claim that the correction has already been confirmed by broad output contraction. Yet Q3 is backward-looking, and employment/income data give plausible forward risks. Strong AI investment can support GDP while future investment returns deteriorate; spending and return on spending are different quantities.
- Quarterly average PCE inflation can decelerate while September monthly/yearly inflation accelerates. Those numbers are not contradictory because their periods and constructions differ.
- One month of weak payrolls is not sufficient to diagnose recession, particularly if labor-force growth has slowed. Falling real income and reliance on investment/wealth effects are reasons to monitor consumption rather than assert GDP durability.

### Gaps
- No October payroll, October CPI or October PCE release was verified; no November realized macro figures should be supplied. Advance GDP and payrolls are revisable, and unrevised vintage values are not final truth.
- No quantitative recession probability can be derived from this record without a specified, validated model.

## What changed since the technology peak, and what remains a risk?

### Takeaway
The clearest measured improvement since the reported October 6 tech closing peak is lower Treasury yields. The Fed has not pivoted to cuts; inflation and public/private financing competition remain constraints. Falling tech prices despite falling nominal yields weaken a single-factor explanation based solely on rising rates.

### Cited Findings
- **September 16 Fed decision:** unanimously increased the funds target 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%. The statement described resilient spending, robust capital investment and elevated inflation. This hike **predates** the October 6 tech peak, so cannot by itself be described as new post-peak information. [Fed September 16 statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm).
- **October 28 Fed decision:** held target at 3.75–4.00%, vote 10–2. Cook and Hammack dissented in favor of another 25bp increase. A hold is not a cut, and the dissent direction matters. [Fed October 28 statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm).
- **Official nominal Treasury par yields, percent per annum:** October 6: 2y 4.79%, 10y 5.27%, 30y 5.64%; October 30: 2y 4.56%, 10y 4.94%, 30y 5.31%. Thus post-peak changes are **-23bp/-33bp/-33bp**. September 30 comparison: 4.88%/5.29%/5.64%, hence month changes -32bp/-35bp/-33bp. These are end-of-day curve-derived nominal par yields, not equity discount rates or real yields. Page latest date is October 30; no November 2 closing yields are claimed. [US Treasury daily table, 2026 selected](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve&field_tdr_date_value=2026).
- **Real yield:** FRED DFII10 latest directly displayed observation October 29 was **2.63%**, updated October 30 at 3:16pm CDT. October 28 was 2.67%. Do not label this an October 30 or November 2 observation. This is a traded TIPS real-yield measure, not nominal yield minus contemporaneous CPI. [FRED DFII10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII10).
- **Credit:** ICE BofA US high-yield option-adjusted spread October 30 **3.44 percentage points (344bp)**, vs October 28 3.14 (314bp), October 29 3.26. FRED update November 2 at 9:06am CST; observation remains October 30. Two-day widening 30bp is an emerging stress signal but not proof of systemic crisis. [FRED BAMLH0A0HYM2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BAMLH0A0HYM2).
- St. Louis Fed president Musalem's **October 30 speech**, explicitly his view rather than committee consensus, says growth remains strong, recent financial tightening was smaller than earlier Liberation Day/Middle East shocks; credit spreads widened especially for AI infrastructure borrowers; financing remains healthy; nominal yields' recent-month increase mostly reflects higher expected real policy rates. He views additional policy firming as likely necessary, while highlighting stagnant real household income and geopolitical risks. [Speech, published October 30](https://www.stlouisfed.org/from-the-president/remarks/2026/remarks-on-us-economy-and-monetary-policy-somc).
- IMF October 13 briefing identifies AI profitability/investment disappointment, higher sovereign yields, and cyber threats as potential triggers amplified by leverage/concentrated exposures; this is risk assessment, not a realized-crisis declaration. [IMF original briefing](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/10/13/tr-101326-press-briefing-transcript-global-financial-stability-report-annual-meetings-2026).

### Inferences
- Treasury relief is quantitatively real, but the simultaneous equity drawdown suggests earnings expectations, AI returns, equity risk premiums, supply constraints and positioning also matter. Correlation cannot identify which driver dominated.
- Inflation changed adversely in information available **after** October 6: CPI published October 14 and PCE October 29; weak September employment was already public October 2. Strong Q3 GDP only became official October 29. Distinguish data publication date from underlying period.
- Long-term yields remaining high in absolute terms and positive real yields can constrain valuations even while nominal yields fall. A market hold/rally cannot be interpreted as restoration of a zero-rate environment.

### Gaps
- No matched October 6 real-yield observation was collected; do not claim a verified real-yield drawdown equal to nominal yield declines. No verified dollar-index series was collected. Do not assert dollar strengthening/weakening as observed fact from these notes.
- HY index is broad corporate credit, not a complete measure of private-credit/data-center financing, and two days are not a robust long-term trend. No default-cycle acceleration established.

## How should midterms, tariffs and fiscal policy enter the outlook?

### Takeaway
The November 3 election is an imminent unresolved catalyst, with policy significance depending on institutional outcomes and surprise versus expectations. Fiscal borrowing pressure and changed tariff law are documented background risks; the midterm calendar is not evidence that stocks must rebound.

### Cited Findings
- **Election timing:** official California Secretary of State page specifies the general election **November 3, 2026**. As-of November 2, results are unavailable; polling, implied probabilities and actual outcomes must not be interchanged. [California Secretary of State](https://www.sos.ca.gov/elections/upcoming-elections/general-election-november-3-2026).
- **Fiscal outcome estimate:** CBO October 8 estimated FY2026 deficit **$2.0 trillion**, $218bn more than FY2025. Revenues +$169bn (3%), outlays +$386bn (6%). Spending rises included interest, benefits, defense, education. This is an early CBO year-end estimate, distinct from Treasury's final statement and from February budget projections. [CBO Monthly Budget Review September 2026, published October 8](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61985).
- **Tariff baseline changed:** CBO August 20 analysis, policies effective through July 31, reports February 20 Supreme Court removal of IEEPA tariff authority; replacement Section 122 tariffs expired July 24 and Section 301 tariffs then took effect. CBO estimated import-weighted effective tariff rate **10%**, versus November 2025 estimate 15% and 2024 2%. July changes raised projected FY2027–2036 deficits $0.9trn relative to February baseline; estimate includes behavioral/policy uncertainty. This is **not** a live November tariff measurement and should be labeled as of July31. [CBO August 20 dated analysis](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62704).

### Inferences
- Divided government could constrain major legislation, but executive trade policy, enforcement, procurement and regulation still matter. Unified control could facilitate legislation without guaranteeing its direction or equity-market impact. Election-day surprise and bond-market response matter more than a calendar-only strategy.
- Policy channels for tech include corporate taxes and expensing, antitrust/AI regulation, semiconductor export restrictions, energy/grid permitting, and budget/debt-ceiling disputes. These are scenario channels, not claims about a newly enacted November law.
- Existing deficit pressure can sustain term-premium/real-financing risk even if near-term growth remains strong. A large deficit is not by itself evidence of imminent fiscal crisis.
- Do not derive odds from the small sample of historical midterms or advertise an automatic post-midterm rally. Historical unconditional average returns omit current valuation, inflation, war, earnings and expectations.

### Gaps
- No reliable current election-win probability, market-implied surprise, legislative package or completed election outcome was verified. Avoid numerical political forecast.
- July31 tariff study is not a substitute for a complete current product/country legal tariff schedule. Exact November tariffs, China/Taiwan export-policy changes and incremental fiscal legislation require separate verification before being described as new catalysts.
- May2026 Fed financial-stability report is older context; the Fed publications page did not supply a verified October/November2026 report. Do not conflate October IMF report with Federal Reserve report.
